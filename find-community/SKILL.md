# Find Community

Help identify the user's community — the foundation of a minimalist business.

## Core Principle

**Start with community, not with a product idea.** The best minimalist businesses are built by people already deeply embedded in a community who notice a problem worth solving.

## Framework: Identify Communities

Walk through these questions:

1. **What communities are you already part of?** Professional groups, hobby communities, online forums, local organizations, identity-based groups, alumni networks, etc.
2. **Where do you spend time online?** Reddit, Discord, Slack groups, X/Twitter, forums, Facebook groups, Substacks, YouTube communities.
3. **What problems do you hear people complain about repeatedly?** Best ideas come from persistent, recurring pain points.
4. **Which communities would you serve for years?** This isn't a weekend project.

## Evaluation Criteria

For each potential community:

- **Are you a genuine member?** Understand the language, values, culture. Contributing, not lurking.
- **Is the problem painful enough to pay for?** Not every problem is a business.
- **Can you reach these people?** Know where they gather? Can you contact them directly?
- **Is it large enough but not too large?** Niche you can dominate, not a market too broad to stand out.

## Key Insight

> Don't start with a business idea. Start with the people. Communities are the starting point. Become a pillar, contribute genuinely, notice what problems persist.

## Anti-patterns

- Inventing a community from scratch rather than joining an existing one
- Choosing a community purely for market size rather than genuine interest
- Skipping participation and jumping straight to "what can I sell"
- Targeting too broad an audience ("everyone who uses the internet")

## Output

Narrow down to 1-3 communities the user could realistically serve:
- The community
- The persistent problem
- How the user is connected
- Where this community gathers (online and offline)
